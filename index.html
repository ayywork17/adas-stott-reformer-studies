Introduction

What is STOTT PILATES?

A contemporary approach to the mind-body exercises pioneered by Joseph H. Pilates, developed by Merrithew. It incorporates modern exercise science and spinal rehabilitation knowledge into more than 500 systematic matwork and equipment-based exercises.

The focus is on core stability — including pelvic and shoulder girdle stabilization, neutral alignment, and breathing — resulting in a balanced, aligned body that looks fit, feels revitalized, and moves with ease.

Benefits

  • Longer, leaner muscles
  • Core strength and stability
  • Injury prevention
  • Relief from stress and back pain
  • Better posture
  • Improved balance and coordination
  • Enhanced athletic performance
  • Effective post-rehabilitation
  • Heightened mind-body awareness and increased self-confidence

Equipment & Safety

Reformer Safety Rules

  • Always ensure at least one spring is attached to the gearbar before getting on
  • The Reformer should be used under supervision of a trained professional
  • Consult a physician before beginning any exercise program
  • Stop immediately if you experience chest pain, faintness, or musculoskeletal discomfort
  • Keep body, clothing and hair free from all moving parts
  • Allow at least two feet of clear space on either side
  • Do not attempt to repair equipment yourself — consult Merrithew
  • Do not allow children to use or be around equipment without adult supervision

Getting into supine on the Reformer

Sit on the carriage with legs off to one side. Reach hand nearest the footbar across body to shoulder rest. Tuck opposite shoulder under arm and roll onto back, lowering head to headrest. Bring legs up and place feet on footbar, lower arms by side.

Key Accessories

  • Mat converter — stable matwork surface
  • Jumpboard / Cardio-Tramp Rebounder — plyometric exercises
  • Rotational Diskboard — controlled internal and external rotation
  • Cross-Bow — ideal alignment for hands, wrists, shoulders
  • Padded platform extender — widens platform; elevates pelvis when sitting
  • Gripper mat — increases traction to prevent slipping
  • Reformer box (long or short side) — facilitates and varies exercises
  • Footstrap — keeps feet secure on the box
BASIC PRINCIPLES

The Five Basic Principles

These five principles are the basis of all STOTT PILATES exercises. They work together not as separate entities but to create intelligent exercise that is both safe and effective.

When teaching each Principle, three key objectives apply: What is the Principle? Why is it beneficial? How can we experience these benefits? Movement health depends on alignment, flexibility, and motor control reflecting quality, control, balance, and force transfer.

Principle 1

Breathing

Inhale through nose (rib cage expands three-dimensionally, posteriorly and laterally). Exhale through pursed lips. Inhale = spinal extension (rib cage opens out/up). Exhale = spinal flexion (rib cage closes in/down). Light TVA and pelvic floor engagement throughout both phases.

Mind-Body Awareness Promotes concentration and awareness of self. Breathing supports the body through motion and helps differentiate optimal from non-optimal movement patterns.

Three-Dimensional Nature Upper rib cage: pump handle motion (anterior/superior). Lower rib cage: bucket handle motion (lateral/superior). Last two ribs: caliper motion (lateral). These motions allow lungs to expand in all directions and promote the ideal length-tension relationship of the deep stabilizers. When motions are blocked, non-optimal patterns result and deep lumbopelvic stabilizers are harder to activate.

Muscle Activation Stabilizer muscles prefer oxygen-based energy. The diaphragm, TVA, and pelvic floor maintain intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) creating optimal lumbopelvic stability similar to a car airbag effect on the spine.

Common breath patterns: Nose breathing filters allergens, warms/moisturizes air, triggers nitric oxide release, and activates the diaphragm more fully. Mouth breathing tends toward a superior vertical breath and overuses accessory muscles (scalenes, trapezius, SCM). Belly breathing is relaxing but alone does not facilitate IAP. STOTT PILATES breathing (inhale through nose + exhale through pursed lips) encourages 3D rib cage motion synced with deep abdominals and pelvic floor; pursed-lip exhale increases abdominal recruitment on expiration.

Instructor note: If rib cage motions are restricted, a superior vertical breath compensates and deep stabilizers are harder to activate. Individual accommodations can be made as needed.

Principle 2

Pelvic Placement

Stabilization of the pelvis and lumbar spine both statically and dynamically in all positions throughout all movements. Two primary positions are used.

Neutral The natural anterior convex curve of the lumbar spine is present; deep stabilizer muscles are in mid-range length. Provides ideal length-tension relationship for muscles to react to forces with appropriate stiffness and control. Not achieved by forcibly arching the back; allow the sacrum to rest on the Mat. With TVA engaged, no strain through lumbar extensors or hip flexors. Neutral is the primary goal and a good base for efficient movement patterns.

Imprint Used when neutral cannot be maintained in open or closed chain exercises. Combines a slight posterior pelvic tilt with lumbar lengthening. Obliques approximate pelvis and rib cage anteriorly, lengthening the lumbar posteriorly. Useful when abdominals are weak or lordosis is present. Do not press the lower back fully into the Mat or tuck using rectus abdominis and gluteals (reduces stability). Goal: decrease imprint amplitude until neutral is achieved with control.

Key exercises: Rocking Pelvis (explore anterior/posterior range; neutral is in between). Neutral to Imprint (TVA stays engaged; obliques shorten toward imprint, lengthen returning to neutral). Leg Slides (pelvic control against peripheral movement; imprint used if neutral cannot be maintained). Leg Lifts and Toe Taps (Toe Taps start in imprint, progress to neutral once stability is demonstrated).

Principle 3

Rib Cage Placement

The rib cage should be stacked over the pelvis so the thoracic and pelvic diaphragms are parallel. Three key beneficial elements.

Bony Connections The rib cage's articular relationship to the thorax provides an indirect assessment of thoracic spine alignment. Movement and alignment in one area affects the other. Static and dynamic rib cage alignment provides a visual reference for establishing neutral position.

Muscular Connections The abdominal wall attaches to the lower ribs and pelvis. Abdominals must be recruited to maintain rib cage, thoracic spine, and pelvis in alignment. The rib cage often lifts in supine or deviates forward in sitting; oblique engagement ensures proper alignment at all times. In kyphosis, abdominals may be shortened.

Physiological Motions of the Spine During active inhalation the spine extends; during active exhalation the spine flexes. Use this relationship to support spinal movements in exercise. During quiet breathing these movements should be minimal. The exhale can be used for abdominal recruitment in any part of a movement for safety and control. Breath must synchronize with movement, facilitating it not fighting it.

Key exercises: Arm Raises and Scissors (reach arms overhead only as far as abdominal connection is maintained and rib cage stays aligned with pelvis). Active Breathing with Physiological Spinal Motion (actively inhale to extend thoracic spine and lift chest; exhale to flex and lower rib cage, illustrating the breath/rib cage/spine interrelationship).

Principle 4

Scapular Movement & Stabilization

Awareness of scapular mobility and stability is important during initiation and performance of every exercise. When stability is absent, there is a tendency to overwork muscles around the neck and shoulders. A sense of width should be maintained across the front and back of the shoulder girdle. The scapulae should lie flat on the rib cage and glide across it without winging away. Two key elements.

Lack of Articular Support The scapulae lack a direct bony attachment to the rib cage and spine, giving them great mobility. They have direct attachments to the clavicles and indirect attachment to the rib cage.

Increased Muscular Support The scapulae rely on axio-scapular muscles as dynamic ligaments, modulating tension to keep scapulae controlled against the rib cage at rest and during arm movements. Movements include Protraction/Retraction, Elevation/Depression, and Upward/Downward Rotation. Movement control and appropriate muscle tension, not rigidity, should be maintained.

Push and Pull patterns: Push involves elbow extension, shoulder flexion, and scapular protraction, activating anterior musculature. Pull involves the opposite, with scapular retraction engaging posterior musculature. Rotation integrates both simultaneously. These patterns are sequences of joint movements developed from birth and necessary in many functional activities.

Key exercises: Scapular Setting on Neutral Spine (elevate, widen elbows, gently depress; arms float down to a tension-free starting position). Scapula Isolations covering Protraction/Retraction and Elevation/Depression (mobilize scapulae in all directions; supine isolates movement, seated adds range and gravitational challenge). Arm Scissors and Arm Circles (awareness of scapular movement with arm movement; elevation/depression with upward/downward rotation).

Instructor note: Observe symmetry of shoulder height; resting scapula placement relative to rib cage and spine; scapulo-humeral rhythm during arm movements; dynamic postural balance in the lumbopelvic, hip, and thoracic spine; differences between bilateral and unilateral movements.

Principle 5

Head & Cervical Placement

The cervical spine should continue the line created by the thoracic spine in neutral and during flexion, extension, lateral flexion, and rotation to provide a biomechanical advantage for supporting the weight of the head and neck. Two biomechanical regions.

Upper Cervical and Head Control (Head + C1-C2) Head weight should balance over the lower cervical spine, shoulders, and torso in neutral. Cervical flexion initiates with a slight head nod (cranio-vertebral flexion, movement of head on C1-2), stimulating deep neck flexors and preventing overactivity of superficial muscles. Cervical extension initiates with a slight chin lift, activating deep cervical extensors. Using the eyes to initiate motion taps into a motor control reflex for proper muscle activation.

Lower Cervical Control (C3-C7) The cervical spine should continue the line of the thoracic spine in all movements. Avoid jamming the chin into the chest during flexion or the back of the head to the base of the neck during extension. In supine, maintain neutral; use pads or cushions under the head if kyphosis or forward head posture is present.

Key exercises: Cranio-Vertebral Flexion / Head Nod (segmental flexion of upper cervical spine; must be done in closed kinetic chain with head on Mat or cushion or superficial muscles will be recruited). Modified Ab Prep (Head Nod incorporated into full upper spine flexion; maintain neck length throughout). Modified Breast Stroke Prep (cervical extension incorporated into full upper spine extension; depress and retract scapulae first to create space; extension is created by the spine and supported by the arms).

Starting Positions

Common Starting Positions on the Reformer

Footwork Focus

Consistent Footwork Focus Points

Remain constant throughout Footwork, Second Position, and Single Leg.

Stabilize pelvis and lumbar in neutral; avoid posterior or anterior tilt
Stabilize rib cage and scapulae throughout; avoid upper body tension
Extend legs fully (except small-range variation) without locking or overextending knees
Maintain alignment through center of hip, knee, and ankle joints
Use vasti to track patella properly (vastus medialis often needs encouragement)
Both legs work equally in Footwork and Second Position
Maintain pelvis level; avoid rotation and lateral tilting
Avoid tension in upper shoulders and neck
Key Muscles

Important Muscles to Know

Transversus Abdominis (TVA)Deepest abdominal layer; wraps horizontally around torso between lower ribs and pelvis. Acts as a girdle for the lumbo-pelvic region. Active throughout both inhale and exhale.
MultifidusDeep segmental spinal muscle. Works with TVA to stabilize the lumbar spine locally. Key to safe, effective exercise.
Pelvic FloorSheet of muscle between pubic bone, coccyx, and ischium. Co-contracts with TVA. Sub-maximal engagement — imagine gently contracting and lifting.
Oblique AbdominalsActivated on exhale; shorten as pelvis and ribs move toward imprint, lengthen toward neutral. Assist TVA.
Serratus AnteriorKeeps medial border of scapula flat on rib cage. Prevents winging. Active during overhead movements and thoracic flexion.
TrapeziusUpper fibers elevate scapula; middle retract; lower depress. Works with rhomboids and serratus for balanced scapular stability.
RhomboidsRetract (pull inward) the scapulae toward the spine. Important in rowing and pulling movements.
Vastus Medialis (VMO)Tracks the patella properly during leg extension. Often undertrained — specifically cued in Footwork.
DiaphragmPrimary breathing muscle. Depresses on inhale allowing abdomen to expand slightly. Efficient gas exchange occurs more in the lower lobes of the lungs.
Landmarks

Key Anatomical Landmarks

ASISAnterior Superior Iliac Spine. Front bony prominence of each hip. In neutral: ASIS and pubis symphysis lie in the same horizontal plane.
Pubis SymphysisJoint at the front of the pelvis between the two pubic bones. Lies in the same horizontal plane as the ASIS in neutral.
Sit Bones (Ischial Tuberosities)Bony points at the base of the pelvis. Primary weight-bearing contact points in seated positions.
SacrumTriangular bone at the base of the spine. Contacts the mat in supine. Should maintain contact in neutral alignment.
Thoracolumbar FasciaDense connective tissue in the back into which the TVA attaches posteriorly. TVA tightening acts as a girdle through this fascia.
CoccyxThe tailbone — lowest section of the spine. One attachment point of the pelvic floor muscles.
Kinetic Chains

Open vs Closed Kinetic Chain

Closed kinetic chain: The distal end (foot or hand) is fixed against a stable surface — e.g. feet on the footbar. Pelvis and spine are ideally neutral.

Open kinetic chain: The distal end is free to move — e.g. legs in straps, arms in straps. Greater stabilization demand. Imprint may be needed until abdominals are strong enough to maintain neutral.

Level Overview

Intermediate Reformer

Builds directly on the Essential level, introducing more complex exercises, increased coordination demands, greater range of motion, new positions (prone on box, kneeling, standing), and more dynamic movement patterns. The same Five Basic Principles and Seven Exercise Essences apply — but with heightened difficulty and subtlety.

Key progression from Essential: Intermediate adds exercises in new positions (kneeling, long-stretch on carriage), introduces dynamic spinal articulation (Long Spine), adds challenge to already-known exercises (e.g. Beats, Frog, Coordination), and introduces standing balance work (Front/Back/Side Splits, Star Prep).

What's new at Intermediate level

  • Beats — lateral rotation and abduction with pelvic stability
  • Frog — hip circle in straps; both directions
  • Stag — asymmetrical leg position in straps
  • Coordination — full-body pattern with arm and leg sequencing
  • Back Rowing and Front Rowing (full) — seated rowing series
  • Backstroke Prep (Long Box) — prone; posterior chain with rotation
  • Feet Pulling Straps (Box) — seated spinal rotation
  • Long Stretch, Down Stretch, Up Stretch — standing on carriage; plank and extension work
  • Elephant (Intermediate) — standing; added mobility demands
  • Semicircle — supine; hip extension and flexion arc with spinal articulation
  • Chest Expansion — kneeling; thoracic extension with rotation
  • Side Arm Preps Kneeling, Side Twist Kneeling, Reverse Expansion — kneeling series
  • Mermaid (Short Box), Lean — short box lateral flexion variations
  • Long Spine — supine in straps; full spinal articulation into inversion
  • Front Splits, Back Splits, Side Splits (Intermediate) — standing balance
  • Star Prep — side-lying; hip abduction and balance challenge
Progression Notes

Key Differences from Essential

Essential Level

  • Supine and seated as primary positions
  • Short Spine (prep + full) in straps
  • Side Splits (basic)
  • Leg Circles (basic)
  • Arms Pulling Straps on Long Box
  • Short Box series (Round, Straight, Twist, Tree)
  • Elephant (basic standing)
  • Stomach Massage (Round Back, Straight Back)

Intermediate Level

  • Kneeling, prone, and standing positions added
  • Long Spine (full inversion articulation)
  • Front, Back and Side Splits (standing balance)
  • Leg Circles (laterally rotated variation)
  • Backstroke Prep; Plow (Long Box prone)
  • Short Box adds Mermaid, Lean, Twist with Round Back
  • Elephant (straighter back, more hip mobility)
  • Stomach Massage adds Reach, Twist variations

ABDOMINALS

Abdominal Muscles

Transversus Abdominis (TA)

No spinal movement — pure stabilizer. Compresses the abdominal cavity and stabilizes the lumbo-pelvic region. The deepest abdominal layer; co-activates with the pelvic floor and diaphragm to create IAP.

Internal Obliques

Involved in stabilizing the trunk. Bilaterally: flexes the lower spine. Unilaterally: rotates spine to the same side; laterally flexes spine to the same side.

External Obliques

Involved in stabilizing the trunk. Bilaterally: flexes the upper spine. Unilaterally: rotates spine to the opposite side; laterally flexes spine to the same side.

Rectus Abdominis

Flexes the spine, laterally flexes the spine, and prevents anterior tilt of the pelvis.

Extends and laterally flexes the spine.

Primary hip extensor and lateral rotator. Upper fibres assist abduction.

Hip abductor and medial rotator. Critical pelvic stabilizer in single-leg stance.

Hip abductor and medial rotator. Assists flexion when hip is flexed.

Basic Principles

Five Basic Principles — Intermediate Application

The same five principles apply at Intermediate level, but with greater demand on each. The body must maintain them through more dynamic, complex, and range-intensive movement.

Principle 1

Breathing

Same pattern — inhale through nose (rib cage expands three-dimensionally), exhale through pursed lips. At Intermediate level, breathing must be maintained through more complex sequences, kneeling and standing positions, and exercises requiring simultaneous arm and leg coordination. Awareness of breath facilitates adaptation to higher physical demands.

Principle 2

Pelvic Placement

Neutral remains the goal. Imprint used when load exceeds abdominal capacity — particularly in open-chain exercises like straps work. New Intermediate exercises (Long Spine, Frog, Long Stretch) specifically challenge the ability to maintain pelvic neutral under greater range and load. Square pelvis is critical in standing exercises (Splits series, Star Prep).

Principle 3

Rib Cage Placement

Must be maintained in new and more demanding positions — prone on box, kneeling, standing. Particularly challenged in thoracic extension exercises (Down Stretch, Chest Expansion). Avoid overextending by depressing or collapsing rib cage on exhale during these movements.

Principle 4

Scapular Stabilization

Greater arm-loading in Intermediate requires scapular stabilization to be robust. Especially important in kneeling exercises (Chest Expansion, Side Arm Preps Kneeling, Reverse Expansion) and prone long-box work. Shoulders should not round forward or elevate during arm movements. Avoid winging of the scapulae.

Principle 5

Head & Cervical Placement

Increased range of thoracic extension in Intermediate exercises means greater demand on cervical alignment. Keep the eyeline appropriate to the degree of extension — avoid cervical overextension. In prone (Long Box), ensure even extension from cervical through lumbar spine.

Pelvic Placement in New Positions

Pelvic & Spine Alignment — Position Notes

  • Standing on carriage (Elephant, Stretches): Keep spine long — neither over-arching nor over-rounding. Abdominal support prevents lumbar sinking as hips extend
  • Kneeling: Pelvis neutral; avoid anterior tilt (common in kneeling due to hip flexor tightness). Scapulae stabilized before arm movement begins
  • Prone on box: Pelvis neutral/imprint; gluteals engage to stabilize; avoid collapsing into lumbar extension
  • Sitting on box: Weight back on sit bones, spine long; roll pelvis from front of femur, not by leaning back
  • Semicircle: Pelvic neutral on press out, articulate on return arc — sequencing of imprint to neutral is the essence

New Exercises — Key Essences

What Each New Exercise Emphasizes

  • Beats: Stability (torso) + Coordination (rhythm and breath with abduction/adduction)
  • Frog: Mobility (hip circles, both directions) + Stability (pelvis stays neutral)
  • Stag: Stability (asymmetrical leg challenge) + Target Muscles (hip flexors, adductors)
  • Coordination: Coordination (simultaneous arm pull + leg sequencing) + Sequencing
  • Long Spine: Mobility (full spinal articulation) + Sequencing (tail to thoracic roll-up) + Balance (on upper thoracic area)
  • Long Stretch: Stability (plank/dart position) + Target Muscles (glutes, scapular stabilizers, abdominals)
  • Down Stretch: Mobility (thoracic extension) + Stability (pelvis and shoulders must not collapse)
  • Up Stretch: Mobility (pike and extension) + Sequencing (spine flexes from head on return)
  • Semicircle: Sequencing (pelvic articulation through arc) + Mobility (hip joint range)
  • Chest Expansion: Stability (torso in kneeling) + Target Muscles (posterior chain, thoracic extensors)
  • Star Prep: Balance (side-lying on carriage) + Target Muscles (hip abductors, lateral stabilizers)
  • Front/Back/Side Splits: Balance (standing on moving carriage) + Stability (pelvis square)

Footwork Focus

Consistent Footwork Focus Points

Same focus points as Essential apply throughout Footwork, Second Position, and Single Leg at Intermediate level.

Stabilize pelvis and lumbar in neutral; avoid posterior or anterior tilt
Stabilize rib cage and scapulae throughout; avoid upper body tension
Extend legs fully (except small-range variation) without locking or overextending knees
Maintain alignment through center of hip, knee, and ankle joints
Use vasti to track patella properly (VMO often needs encouragement)
Both legs work equally in Footwork and Second Position
Maintain pelvis level; avoid rotation and lateral tilting
Avoid tension in upper shoulders and neck
Key Muscles

Muscles Particularly Emphasized at Intermediate

Gluteus MaximusHip extensors — heavily loaded in Long Stretch, Down Stretch, and Semicircle. Prevents pelvis from sagging toward floor in plank-based exercises.
AdductorsInner thigh muscles — central to Beats (adduct to close legs) and Frog. Must work eccentrically on abduction and concentrically on adduction.
Lateral Rotators of HipMaintain lateral rotation of legs in Beats, Frog, Stag. Prevent medial collapse of hip during straps work.
Erector SpinaeBack extensors — more prominent role in Down Stretch, Chest Expansion, prone Long Box work. Must work with abdominals to stabilize thoracic spine in extension.
Gastrocnemius / SoleusCalf complex — Semicircle includes heel lift; Down Stretch and standing exercises require calf control. Running targets eccentric loading of the Achilles.
PsoasHip flexor — maintains sitting position in rowing series. Psoas is active to prevent the torso collapsing back in Front Rowing exercises.
Oblique AbdominalsMore prominently recruited in rotational exercises (Twist, Coordination, Feet Pulling Straps). Resist spinal extension in Long Stretch.
Hip Abductors (Glute Med)Key stabilizers in standing exercises (Splits series, Star Prep). Prevent pelvis from dropping on the stance side.
HamstringsEccentrically control hip extension in Short Spine and Long Spine. Concentrically assist in hip extension phases of Semicircle and bridging.
Position-Specific Anatomy

Body Position Focus Notes

  • Plank / Dart position (Long Stretch): Abdominals + gluteals + scapular stabilizers all fire simultaneously to maintain one straight line from head to heel. Avoid pelvic sag or pike.
  • Kneeling exercises (Chest Expansion): Anterior tilt is common due to shortened hip flexors — actively lengthen hip flexors to achieve neutral pelvis. Scapulae must be set before pulling straps.
  • Semicircle: Requires sequential spinal articulation similar to Short Spine but adds the arc of hip movement. Gluteals and hamstrings must work eccentrically on descent, concentrically on press out.
  • Long Spine: The most complex straps exercise at Intermediate. Requires full sequential spinal roll-up from tail to upper thoracic without the feet moving in space. Balance occurs on upper thoracic area.
  • Star Prep: Side-lying balance on a moving carriage. Top hip abductors stabilize pelvis, bottom obliques prevent side-bending toward carriage.
